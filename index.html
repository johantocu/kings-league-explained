Kings League, Explained: The Football League Built Like a Video Game

Seven a side, 36 minutes, secret cards and a giant dice. How it works, and why millions tune in.

By Johan Torres·7 min read·Rules verified 18 Nov 2026

Picture a Tuesday night five-a-side game, then hand the rulebook to a room full of streamers. That's more or less the Kings League. Gerard Piqué, the former Barcelona and Spain defender, presented it on 10 November 2022: seven against seven, short halves, and rules that sound like they came out of a video game menu. Coaches hold secret cards. A giant dice decides how many players stay on the pitch. Team presidents can walk down from the stands and take a penalty.

It works because it lives where people already watch. Every match streams free on Twitch, YouTube and TikTok, and on the channels of presidents who bring their own millions of followers. In the first season in Spain, a single matchday-three game passed 1.3 million viewers. I'm Colombian and I come from five-a-side football (I'm building a booking platform for small-sided games in Bogotá), so I paid close attention when Colombia made the final of the Kings World Cup Nations in January 2025. We lost 6–2 to Brazil in Turin. Still, a final.

0'–5'

Scale-Up: from 1v1 to 7v7

It starts 1v1 plus keepers: the ball drops from a cage and both players sprint for it. Every minute each side adds a player: 2v2, 3v3, and so on until it's 7v7 at minute 5.

The deck

The secret cards

Before kick-off each coach draws one card at random. Nobody knows what the other bench is holding. Tap a card to flip it.

Minute 20

Roll the dice

The second half starts with a giant dice. It has three faces, each printed twice: a logo, a 2 and a 3. Whatever lands decides how many outfield players each team keeps for three minutes.

Each result has the same chance: 2 in 6.

From Barcelona to the world

The analysis

Why it works

01Built for clips

A normal match asks for two hours of your attention. A Kings League game gives you a reason to look up every few minutes: the Scale-Up, a secret card, double goals, the dice, Matchball. Each one is a ready-made clip. That suits the way a lot of Gen Z follows sport, in short pieces on a phone, on TikTok and YouTube as much as on a TV. You can catch the last five minutes and still feel you saw the whole story.

02Presidents are the distribution

The presidents are the marketing. When Ibai or Gaules streams a match, the game lands on a channel whose audience was already there. The league doesn't have to build a fan base from zero in every country; it borrows one and turns it into a rivalry. It's also how the format travels. Italy, Brazil, France, Germany and MENA each got their own local creators and ex-players as presidents, not imports from Spain.

03Fans hold the controller

Then there's the vote. Fans have shaped the rules from the start, and the newest card, Reverse Penalty, won a public vote for 2025/26. That turns viewers into stakeholders. Like a patch in a video game, the rules shift between seasons and people tune in to see how the new meta plays out. Which is also why this guide has a date on it.
